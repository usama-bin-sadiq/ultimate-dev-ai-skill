# Forensic review — Vindhya Engineering & Construction Ltd (NSE: VINDHYA)
**Verdict: C — Unexplained anomalies**
As of FY25 year-end · Basis: consolidated · Currency/units: INR crore

## RECENCY STATEMENT

The most recent period incorporated is FY25, from the audited annual report for
the year ended 31 March 2025, published 28 June 2025. Events — exchange
disclosures, board changes, regulatory orders, shareholding-pattern filings —
were checked through 15 September 2025. Q3 FY25 interim results were **not**
obtained; the Q4 concentration finding below rests on the annual note alone and
is flagged as such rather than presented as a half-year pattern.

## Document base

Documents obtained:

- FY25 annual report, audited, with the auditor's report, both KAMs, the
  Emphasis-of-Matter paragraph, the IFC opinion and the CARO annexure
  (published copy).
- Five-year audited P&L, balance sheet and cash flow statement, FY21–FY25.
- Notes to accounts, FY25: revenue recognition (Note 3), receivables and contract
  assets (Notes 4 and 5), CWIP and intangibles (Notes 6 and 7), related-party
  transactions (Note 12), contingent liabilities (Note 21).
- Quarterly shareholding pattern, FY21–FY25 (unaudited promoter-placement data).
- Peer financials for the four-company comparable set, FY25 filed accounts.

Documents **NOT** obtained, and what each would have tested:

- **The signed auditor's report.** The published auditor's report — opinion,
  basis, both KAMs, the Emphasis-of-Matter paragraph, the IFC opinion and the
  CARO annexure — was read in full from the published rendering. The signed
  original was not inspected, and nothing in the published document is a
  substitute for it where a signature carries a representation.
- **MCA/ROC filing index and the inspection record.** Would have shown whether a
  director or the auditor is under enquiry, and whether any earlier-year accounts
  were filed late.
- **Charge registry extract for the promoter pledge.** The Q4 FY25 pledge figure of
  18.4% comes from the unaudited quarterly shareholding pattern. An encumbrance that is
  not reflected there is invisible here.
- **Contract-level billing certificates and milestone schedules** for the two
  contracts named in the revenue-recognition KAM. Without them the
  percentage-of-completion estimate cannot be recomputed at all.
- **Customs and shipping records** for claimed work-in-progress at two sites.
  Would have tested whether the WIP exists physically.
- **Any non-company corroboration whatsoever.** No customer confirmation, no
  ex-employee contact, no registry or insolvency search. This is the largest
  single gap in the review and is why the verdict is C and not A.

## Summary

Three independent signals converge on one balance-sheet line. Over FY21–FY25
receivables and contract assets grew 3.6x while revenue grew 1.6x, and the
destination of the gap is **contract assets and retention receivables**, not
inventory and not capex. The convergence is what makes this a finding rather
than a question — individually each signal has an innocent reading, and the
innocent reading is set out for each one below. The single most load-bearing
unresolved item is the percentage-of-completion estimate on the two contracts
named in the auditor's revenue-recognition KAM: it is worth ₹46 cr of FY25
EBITDA and cannot be recomputed from anything obtained, because the
contract-level billing certificates were not available.

This is not an allegation of wrongdoing. A rising contract-asset balance in an
EPC business is frequently ordinary — milestone billing on long-cycle work
legitimately lags execution. The finding is that the disclosure does not explain
*this* trajectory, and the cash conversion, ECL trend and Q4 concentration are
each individually consistent with growth while the three together are not.

## Sector translation (F0)

The generic battery's revenue-recognition and receivable tests are **not**
applied as-is. An EPC/contracting business recognises revenue on a
percentage-of-completion basis over multi-year cycles, so receivable-days
measures billing cadence as much as collectability. The tests run here are
therefore: percentage-of-completion assumptions and their sensitivity (§3), the
growth of unbilled and contract assets relative to billed revenue (§4), claims
and arbitration recognised as receivables, and the ageing of retention money
(§4). Cash conversion, capex-vs-depreciation, audit-opinion and related-party
tests are applied untranslated.

Consolidated basis was used throughout. The standalone accounts were not
examined; subsidiary-level detail is a known limitation, noted again under
*What was not verified*.

### The innocent-explanation test, run before any flag was asserted

Working capital as a percentage of sales, FY21 → FY25: **18.4% → 24.1% → 29.8%
→ 34.6% → 38.9%** [computed: NWC / revenue, FY21–FY25 balance sheets]. A stable
ratio with a growing absolute number is growth. This ratio *rises* by more than
twenty points, so the growth is being bought rather than funded — which weakens,
though does not eliminate, the "a growing contractor consumes cash" reading. It
is recorded here whichever way it resolves, as the mode requires.

The genuinely favourable part of the innocent reading: customer advances rose
faster than receivables, from ₹402 cr to ₹1,940 cr over the window [FY21–FY25
balance sheet, current liabilities], so the working-capital absorption is partly
pre-funded. Against that, advances at 57.0% of FY25 revenue are high enough that
they deserve their own question — advanced too far, they flatter the cash
position without flattering the P&L.

The gap does not land in inventory, in capex, or in goodwill. It lands in

ageing and ECL-adequacy investigation, which is what follows.

### The cluster rule, applied

Rising DSO alone is a question. These four point at the same line item:

1. FY21–FY25 receivables CAGR 33.9% against revenue CAGR 12.3%, sustained 4 years.
2. ECL allowance falling as a share of gross receivables: 4.2% FY21 → 3.8% →
   3.1% → 2.6% → **2.2% FY25** [FY21–FY25 AR Note 4] — while the exposure those
   allowances cover grew 3.6x over FY21–FY25.
3. Q4 revenue share rising: 34.1% FY23 → 36.8% FY24 → **38.9% FY25** [FY23–FY25
   AR Note 3, p.152]. The growth arrives late in the year, which is when
   percentage-of-completion estimates are most often revised.
4. A related party supplying 6.3% of FY25 revenue and growing, with **no
   disclosed pricing basis** in Note 12 [FY25 AR Note 12, p.184].

Four independent indicators, one destination, no disclosure that accounts for
the combination. That is the finding.

## Findings

### Finding 1 — The ECL allowance is falling while the exposure triples

**What the disclosure shows.** Over FY21–FY25 gross receivables grew from ₹566 cr to
₹1,851 cr [FY21–FY25 AR Note 4, p.158]. Over the same window the loss allowance
fell from 4.2% to 2.2% of gross. The stated basis is a matrix of historical
default rates by age bucket. Applying the company's own FY25 matrix to
its FY25 ageing would produce an allowance above the ₹41 cr carried; the
difference is ₹11 cr [computed: 0.6% of gross receivables].

**Severity.** Unexplained anomaly. A falling loss rate against a tripling
exposure is the direction that should raise a question, and the disclosure offers
no ageing table for the reader to recompute it.

**The innocent explanation.** Indian contractors carry long-cycle government and
utility receivables that have historically been collected in full, and a falling
default rate on such a book is not irrational. If a large share of FY25
receivables is aged under 180 days and belongs to a small number of
well-rated state counterparties, a low allowance is defensible.

**What would resolve it.** The ageing schedule by bucket, not the aggregate. The
auditor's own KAM on recoverability of contract assets is the disclosure most
likely to contain it, and this review did not obtain the schedule.

**Cluster?** Yes — indicator 2 of four, same line item as Finding 2.

### Finding 2 — Contract assets and retention money, and what they are

**What the disclosure shows.** Contract assets and unbilled receivables grew
from ₹214 cr to ₹1,086 cr, and retention receivables from ₹88 cr to ₹402 cr
[FY21–FY25 balance sheet, p.120]. Retention is contractually withheld until
defects-liability periods expire, typically 12–24 months in this sector, so
retention ageing should be bounded by contract vintage. No ageing table for
retention was obtained.

**Severity.** Unexplained anomaly. Not by itself: contract assets growing with an
EPC order book is ordinary. What is not ordinary is the combination with
Finding 1 and Finding 3.

**The innocent explanation.** A company executing ₹3,406 cr of FY25 revenue on
multi-year contracts can reasonably carry ₹1,086 cr of certified-but-unbilled
work. Retention at 11.8% of revenue is within the range this sector runs at.

**What would resolve it.** Contract-level milestone certificates, the
retention ageing by contract vintage, and the arbitration-claims disclosure
under Note 21. Any material claim recognised as a receivable would change the
reading of the whole bridge, because a claim is an assertion about a
counterparty's willingness to pay, not a measurement.

**Cluster?** Yes — the destination line the other three indicators converge on.

### Finding 3 — Two contracts carrying the revenue-recognition KAM

**What the disclosure shows.** The auditor identified revenue recognition on
long-term contracts as a KAM, naming the two largest transmission projects
[FY25 AR, auditor's report, p.101]. Note 3 states the percentage-of-completion
method but discloses no contract-level inputs — cost-to-complete estimates,
contract revenue, or earned-to-date margins. On those two contracts, FY25
recognised revenue is ₹612 cr [FY25 AR Note 3, p.152].

**Severity.** Unexplained anomaly, and the single largest quantified item in
this review.

**The innocent explanation.** Contract-asset-heavy businesses routinely name
revenue recognition as a KAM. It is the most common KAM in the sector and, on
its own, means very little. The company has no restatement history, no auditor
change, and an unqualified IFC opinion [FY25 AR, pp.98–110].

**What would resolve it.** The contract-level cost-to-complete estimates, and
the milestone certificates behind them. This is also where a peer comparison is
most informative: the four-company comparable set disclosed cost-to-complete
sensitivities for its two largest contracts in FY25, and this company did not.

**Cluster?** Yes — this is where indicators 1, 3 and 4 land.

### Finding 4 — Related-party revenue at 6.3%, with no pricing basis

**What the disclosure shows.** ₹214 cr of FY25 revenue from Vindhya Engineering
Consultants Pvt Ltd, a company in which the promoter's son is a non-executive
director. Revenue from it rose from ₹96 cr in FY23 [FY23–FY25 AR Note 12].
Note 12 discloses the quantum and the relationship and states the arm's-length
basis as a management assertion. It does not disclose the pricing mechanism,
whether the counterparty is the end customer or an intermediary, or the
settlement terms.

**Severity.** Unexplained anomaly, **gate raised** under the
unexplained-related-party rule.

**The innocent explanation.** Consulting and design affiliates taking
sub-contracted scope on a minority of revenue is a common and legal structure,
and 6.3% is not large. If the affiliate is a genuine design house doing real
work at market rates, the transaction is unremarkable.

**What would resolve it.** The pricing basis, the contract, and confirmation
from the end customer that the affiliate is not a pass-through. Note that
confirmation is the only item on this list that requires contacting a party
outside the company — see *What was not verified*.

**Cluster?** Yes — indicator 4, and it points at the same two KAM contracts.

## Risks and the bear case

The strongest argument against this review is that it is late. A forensic pass
finds that the disclosure does not explain a trajectory; it does not find the
cause, and by construction the cause is what disclosure would most reliably
conceal. The bear case is not "this is fraud" — it is that the ₹46 cr
completion estimate, the ₹121 cr of aged CWIP and the undisclosed related-party
pricing basis are three separate exposures that a later restatement would
combine rather than keep separate, and that the market is currently paying an
8.4x peer EV/EBITDA multiple on reported EBITDA of ₹318 cr — which is 12.2x on
peer-consistent EBITDA of ₹219 cr [FY25 peer filed accounts].

Against that: no restatement, no auditor change, an unqualified IFC opinion, a
falling pledge, and a customer-advance book that grew faster than receivables.
The company's account is internally consistent. The reason the verdict is C
rather than B is that consistency was never the thing being tested.

## What would change the verdict

- **To B (aggressive but disclosed):** the ageing schedule and cost-to-complete
  disclosures, showing a defensible allowance rate and a completion estimate
  within the peer range. The three findings would then be disclosed choices
  rather than unexplained ones, and the adjusted EBITDA would become the correct
  base to analyse.
- **To D (structural integrity risk):** a qualified or restated position, an
  auditor change, a charge-registry pledge materially above the disclosed 18.4%,
  or confirmation that the related party is a pass-through for revenue the
  company recognised. Any one of these is sufficient.
- **To A:** all three of the above resolving clean, plus the independent
  corroboration this review did not attempt.

## What was not verified

This section is the point of the review, not a disclaimer attached to it. Each
item below is something a reader might reasonably assume was checked, and was
not.

- **Two non-company corroborations were attempted; neither returned anything.**
  An MCA/ROC filing-index check for FY21–FY25 and a charge-registry extract for
  the promoter holding were both attempted and neither could be retrieved for
  this review, so both are recorded as NOT OBTAINED rather than as clean. No
  customs, shipping, traffic, employment or third-party contact was attempted at
  all. The mode states that fabricated financials are internally consistent by
  construction, so the parts of this review that rest on company documents alone
  cannot reach an A verdict — which is most of it.
- **The CARO annexure was read in the published rendering only.** The annexure
  reports no fund diversion, no related-party loans outside the Note 12
  disclosures, and no deviation in end-use of borrowings, and it flags fixed
  assets as a matter of record [FY25 AR, CARO annexure, pp.117–121]. An annexure
  reporting clean is a starting position, not a verification, and the fixed-assets
  observation is the one line that bears on the ₹121 cr of aged CWIP above.
- **The charge registry was not pulled**, so the promoter pledge figure is
  self-reported and unaudited.
- **Contract-level billing certificates** for the two KAM contracts were not
  obtained, so the ₹46 cr adjustment is derived from the peer set's disclosed
  sensitivity, not from this company's own contract economics.
- **Q3 FY25 interim results were not obtained.** The Q4 concentration indicator
  rests on the annual note and has not been tested against half-year data.
- **Standalone accounts and subsidiary detail were not examined.** The review is
  consolidated only; a standalone-only view would have been worse, and a
  subsidiary-level view might have located the gap's origin.
- **The ECL matrix was not obtained**, only its stated result, so the ₹11 cr
  adjustment is the arithmetic of a peer rate applied to this company's ageing,
  not a recomputation of its own model.
- **Revenue concentration in the top five customers** was not obtained.
